Research

Sticky Deposits, Leverage Relief, and Bank Lending

Job market paper

Abstract

I study how bank lending responds to temporary leverage relief when deposits are sticky. Methodologically, I embed deposit stickiness in a euro-area-calibrated general equilibrium model with heterogeneously capitalized banks that face idiosyncratic loan and deposit shocks, a leverage ratio requirement, and equity issuance costs. Leverage relief temporarily excludes reserves from the leverage ratio. In the model, leverage relief removes the risk of equity issuance while it lasts, causing undercapitalized banks to lend more. Because these banks are no longer forced to issue, they also accumulate less equity. Counterintuitively, this reduction in equity also boosts lending. As an undercapitalized bank loses equity, issuance at the end of the relief becomes near-certain. A loss then raises the amount issued as much as a gain lowers it, so the bank weighs gains and losses equally. As undercapitalized banks supply more loans, returns to lending fall and well-capitalized banks lend less. Four quarters of leverage relief increase bank lending by up to 0.9% above steady state, but it falls to 0.6% below steady state once the relief ends, because banks hold less equity. Lastly, I show that leverage relief is more effective after an inflow of deposits into the banking system.

Nominal Corporate Debt, Default, and Inflation

Abstract

This paper investigates how the effectiveness of interest rate policy on prices changes if firms can file for bankruptcy and restructure their debt out-of-court before uncertainty realizes in a two-agent general equilibrium model with money, non-diversifiable idiosyncratic firm risk and nominal debt. I find that if a small, but not-too-small fraction of firms file for bankruptcy, interest rate policy is most effective. A larger interest rate always increases debt-to-output. This reduces the wealth of firm owners when they do not file for bankruptcy, but does not affect their wealth when they do file for bankruptcy. As bankruptcy states are those where labour is least productive, this raises real equilibrium wages, as firm owners are willing to pay more for labour. The higher wages directly increase the wage bill. Indirectly, labour supply increases, pushing it down further. In turn, prices fall as working capital needs to be borrowed, taking demand out of the economy.
